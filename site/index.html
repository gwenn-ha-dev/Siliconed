The adaptive engine

It measures
before it draws.

About a minute. No model. No guesswork. A little suspense.

Before the first render after each install or update, Siliconed gets to know your Mac. It times what each engine does, the GPU, the CPU's matrix unit (AMX) and the Neural Engine, alone and together, on matrix products and attention of every shape the models are made of, in fp32, in bf16 and on published 8-bit weights. Every output is checked against the exact result.

That becomes your Mac's map: what each engine computes, and how much of its speed it keeps beside the others. Each computation of a render is then split over the engines by that map, down to the rows of each matrix product. No route is chosen by chip name: another Mac, or a newer chip, draws its own map. How sure the measure is of each figure is written beside it, and a measure always gives a map. The speed changes; the picture does not move beyond the arithmetic it checked. Help › Measure This Mac… shows it.

The Neural Engine

As fast, or faster.
16 to 26 % less energy.

Where your Mac's map gives it rows, the Neural Engine takes part of the denoiser's matrix products beside the GPU, shape by shape. It computes in fp16: each weight column and each activation row is lifted by a power of two into fp16's range, then brought back in fp32, and rows that overflow go back to the GPU. On int8 weights it reads them as published and widens them itself. Your choice, per model: off by default on the Standard, which stays the reference-checked fp32 image; on by default on Compact and Light. The label in the rack reads computes fp32 · ANE when it is on; a switch in the DiT's box turns it on or off.

Editing

Say it.
It's done.

One to three images, one sentence.

“Convert this photo to a western comic book style with cel shading.” “Put the dog of image 2 next to her.” Qwen-Image-2.1's encoder sees the images. Then drag the curtain across the result and its original to see exactly what changed, or flip between the two with Switch.

Change one thing.
Only that.

“Replace her wetsuit with a white linen blouse.” Same face, same hair, same board, same sea and sun: the instruction touches what it names and leaves the rest alone.

Why Siliconed

Honest math.
Real speed.

Checked against the original, a bit obsessively

Every stage is compared channel by channel with diffusers and transformers in fp32, and rounds where they round. What cannot be checked against the reference does not ship.

34 GB of model, 16 GB of Mac

It sounds like a bad idea; it works. Weights stream from disk as needed. Never more than ¾ of the Mac's memory minus 2 GB (10 GB on a 16 GB Mac): each render computes what it needs before it starts.

Eight bits against eight bits

Z-Image Turbo Compact (GGUF Q8_0), 1024², 8 steps, Faster Rendering and the Neural Engine both on, as they are for the Compact: 81–83 s on an M1 Pro. Draw Things' engine with its default 8-bit q8p, the same day on the same Mac: 135–144 s (23 October 2026, app 0.14). Wall-clock, not identical arithmetic: the samplers and the images differ.

Private by construction

Nothing leaves your Mac but the model downloads you accepted and what you send to another Mac of yours, encrypted. It works offline once a model is installed, and the render cache is gone when you quit.

Scriptable, agents included

silicontrol drives the running app from a terminal, with JSON lines out; silicontrol help is all a script or a coding agent needs.

Reproducible to the bit

Same prompt, seed, settings and version on the same Mac: the same image, byte for byte.

A prompt writer, if you want one (beta)

The Prompt Lab (Window › Prompt Lab) rewrites a short prompt the way each model likes it, or describes an image you drop on it. The writer is Qwen-Image-2.1's own text encoder, made to write, so there is nothing more to download but its 1.25 GB output layer. It keeps every element of your prompt, never moralizes, and steps aside the moment an image starts rendering. Its greedy output matches transformers, token for token.

Render on another Mac

The MacBook in the living room computes on the Mac mini next door: Render › Compute On moves the whole window there. Paired once with a six-digit code, TLS with a key per Mac.

Faster Rendering, the honest trick

It is a choice per model, like the Neural Engine: on for the Compact and the Light, off for the Standard, which stays the image checked against the reference. From 1024 px on each side, the first steps run on a half-size grid, the rest at full size: 17 to 31 % off the clock at 1024² (7 % on MOSS-Image), still all 8 evaluations of the recipe. The trick is in the noise: the small grid gets the averaged picture plus the low band of the noise, and the step brought back to full size clears the fine noise exactly as a full step would, so no grain, no cracks; freckles and book spines survive. Another picture than the full-size one, not a blurrier one. Switch it off and you get the full-size recipe, bit for bit, the one checked against diffusers. The choice travels with the image, so Render Again gives the same one.

Community

Your checkpoints. Your LoRAs.
Your Mac.

Nobody's looking over your shoulder.

Drop in a Civitai checkpoint of a known family (⌘O): it imports as is and borrows its family's encoder and VAE, installed for it if they are not there yet. LoRAs too. Siliconed censors no prompt and adds no filter of its own; nothing goes to a server, and the render cache is emptied at launch and at quit. Whatever you're into stays between you and your GPU.

Each model's license still applies: Krea 2 Community's, for one, asks whoever deploys the model for a content filter.

Install

Pick your size.

Standard, Compact or Light.

The publisher's weights, 8-bit versions, or a 4-to-6-bit transformer. The 8-bit Compact is preselected: as fast or faster on an M1 Pro, half the disk. Every file is pinned to a revision and checked by sha256, and downloads resume where they stopped. The next file downloads while the previous one is prepared.
